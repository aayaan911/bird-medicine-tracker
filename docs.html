The question everyone asks

How to build immunity

There is no monthly course that makes a bird immune. Here is what the research actually says, and what to do instead.

First, the correction. Antibodies are made by the bird's own immune system when it meets an antigen, through vaccination or real exposure. No supplement creates an antibody. What nutrition does is decide how well the bird can mount that response. Correcting a deficiency raises antibody output sharply. Megadosing a bird that is already adequate does not raise it further, and fat soluble vitamins accumulate.
What the research does support. In poultry trials, vitamin E supplemented birds produced antibodies earlier and in greater quantity after vaccination, and vitamin E deficient birds showed depressed antibody production. Vitamin A deficiency reduces the ability to synthesise specific antibodies. Vitamin C improves antibody mediated response in birds under stress. So the nutrients matter, as insurance against deficiency, not as a booster.

What actually keeps birds from getting sick, ranked

1

Biosecurity

The single biggest factor. Four week quarantine for every new bird, in a separate room, handled last every day. Wash hands between rooms. No contact with pigeons or wild birds. Most aviary outbreaks walk in through the door on a new bird or on your own hands.

2

Dry hygiene

Change tray paper daily and wipe trays dry. Remove uneaten soft food within two hours. Dry every drinker and bowl daily. Trichomonas dies when dry. Most bacterial load in an aviary comes from wet droppings and standing water, not from the air.

3

Nutritional adequacy

Vitamin A, E, C, zinc and selenium are the immune nutrients. The goal is no deficiency, not maximum dose. A complete daily multivitamin plus a proper seed and soft food base covers all of them. More does not mean stronger.

4

Gut flora

A probiotic five days at the start of every month, and five days after any antibiotic course. Good bacteria physically occupy the gut so pathogens cannot colonise. This is the one routine monthly course that is genuinely supported.

5

Low stress

Heat above 32°C, crowding, constant disturbance and sudden noise all raise stress hormones, and stress hormones suppress immune function directly. A calm, ventilated, cool room does more than any bottle.

6

Vaccination

Where a vaccine exists for a disease in your region, such as pox, it is the only thing on this list that creates real antibodies. Ask an avian vet what is available locally.

Why a monthly 7 day medicine course is a bad idea

  • Antibiotics and antiprotozoals given routinely breed resistance. When you actually need the drug for a sick bird, it no longer works
  • They kill gut flora, which removes the protection described in point 4 above
  • They mask early symptoms, so you find disease later and sicker
  • A healthy flock on a monthly antimicrobial is being treated for nothing
If someone recommends a product you cannot verify. Ask for the manufacturer's own page, the composition, and the dose per litre. If none of those exist in writing, do not put it in your birds on a monthly schedule. An unverifiable product run 12 times a year is 12 chances to cause harm you cannot diagnose.
The honest summary. If you already run a daily multivitamin, a monthly probiotic, proper quarantine, dry trays and a cool ventilated room, you are already running the best immunity programme available. There is nothing to add. The next gain is not another product, it is doing these six consistently.

Sources: Centennial Review: Effects of vitamins A, D, E and C on the chicken immune system, Poultry Science · Vitamin A deficiency impairs mucosal immune function in chicks, PMC

Read before any dose

Three rules that prevent most harm

Most supplement damage in an aviary comes from stacking and from timing, not from underdosing.

1. Never combine on the same day

  • Two fertility products, such as Fetura 12 with Ferti-Vit, Nekton E or Nekton Breed Star
  • Two vitamin D3 products. Calciform, Calcivet, Avigold Advance, Breed Star and A21 all carry D3
  • Two electrolyte products, such as Spark Liquid with Nekton Elektrolyt or Prolyte-C
  • Any probiotic during a course of medication
2. Stop at the first egg. Nekton E and Nekton Breed Star are pre breeding products. Nekton's own label says to stop the day the first egg is laid, because continuing drives the male too hard and disrupts incubation. Running them through incubation is a known way to lose a clutch.
3. The water rule. Medicated water must be the only water source, mixed fresh every day, and left in the cage no longer than six hours, or three hours on a hot afternoon. Never withhold drinking water overnight to force feeding, that risks dehydration and egg binding.
Why D3 stacking matters. Vitamin D3 is fat soluble and accumulates. Chronic excess causes calcium deposits in soft tissue including the kidneys. Delivered dose per litre at label rate: a high strength liquid D3 product can carry four times what a calcium product carries, so it is the one that must never be doubled.

Timing

The feeding system

Timing decides whether birds actually eat the supplements you paid for.

Time
Action
5:00 PM
Seed pot out. Plain water stays in the cage all night. Never withhold water
6:00 AM
Plain water out. Medicated water in. Soft food in. Birds are hungry and thirsty at the same moment, so they take both
Within 2 hours
Soft food out, whether finished or not. One hour in hot or wet weather
Max 6 hours
Medicated water out and replaced. Three hours on a hot afternoon
Evening
Seed pot back in, fresh. Cycle repeats
Why the two hour rule is not optional. Boiled egg and soft food at 30°C in high humidity is a bacterial culture medium. Leaving it until midday is how loose droppings, crop infections and dead chicks start. Anything not eaten in two hours was never going to be eaten.
Soft food quantities. Non breeding pairs: one boiled egg plus a half spoon of rearing formula plus herbs. Breeding pairs: two eggs, twice daily. Resting hens: two eggs per week only, no rearing formula, or she comes back into condition too early.
The room

Environment targets

Nutrition decides fertility. Environment decides hatch. These are the numbers that matter.

Temperature
Keep below 32°C. Above that, poultry research shows damage to sperm producing cells and falling testosterone. Heat sterility in males is real and reversible
Humidity
Target VPD 9 to 15 hPa during incubation, not a raw humidity percentage. VPD accounts for temperature, raw RH does not
Ventilation
10 to 15 air changes per hour, CO2 under 3,000 ppm. A fan can only bring indoor humidity down to outdoor humidity, never below it
Ammonia
Your nose detects it at about 5 ppm. If you smell it at 6 AM the answer is daily dry trays, not a longer fan cycle. Ammonia comes from wet droppings, not from air
Light
12 to 14 hours, consistent. Set lights to bracket the natural day so the total stays constant as daylight shortens. Do not extend for more activity, it over drives hens
UVB
Glass blocks 100% of UVB. Indoor birds make zero natural vitamin D3. Either a UVB lamp or a dietary D3 product, one or the other, not neither
Nest box
Box humidity always runs above room humidity because of the hen breathing in a sealed cavity. Six to eight small vent holes near the top. Thin dry nesting. No bath water during incubation

The dead egg tells you what went wrong

The stage at which the embryo died is the diagnosis. Open every failed egg and write down the stage.

Died at
Cause
Clear, no development
Not fertile. Male anatomy, vent feathers, perch grip, or heat sterility. Not a nutrition problem
Day 1 to 3, blood ring
Fertility marginal, vitamin E or selenium short, or the egg was chilled or shaken
Day 7 to 14, part formed
Nutrition gap or overheating
Day 18 to 23, fully formed, never pipped
Humidity too high. The commonest killer. Air cell too small, chick cannot breathe. Also thin shell or CO2
Pipped then died
Humidity too low, chick shrink wrapped in the membrane. Or oxygen and CO2
Malposition, curled toes
B vitamin shortage, or incubation temperature swings
The air cell check, in plain terms. An egg breathes through its shell. As water leaves, the empty space at the fat end grows. That space is the chick's first breath before it pips. Candle in a dark room, pencil a line on the shell where the air cell ends. Day 7 about 1/8 of the egg. Day 14 about 1/4. Day 18 about 1/3. Smaller than 1/3 at day 18 means too humid. Much bigger means too dry. Day 18 is the last candle, never after.
New birds

Quarantine, four weeks, no shortcuts

Imported and newly bought birds arrive dehydrated, immune suppressed and often carrying something latent that flares under transport stress.

Week
What to do
Week 1
Separate room. Electrolyte and rehydration only. Warmth. Minimal handling. Let the bird eat and drink and settle
Week 2
Add a daily multivitamin and light soft food. Watch droppings, breathing and weight daily
Weeks 3 and 4
Any treatment course indicated by what you have actually observed. Probiotic five days afterwards
Every day of all four weeks
Handle quarantine birds last. Wash hands and change footwear before returning to the main flock
Never
Move a new bird into breeding before four weeks are complete. Rushing an import is the most common way to lose one
If several new birds die across separate purchases from different sellers, the common factor is not the sellers. It is the receiving process. That is a protocol problem with a known fix, not bad luck.
